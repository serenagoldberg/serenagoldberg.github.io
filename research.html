Generosity vs. Access: The Effects of Subsidy Policy on Price and Quality of Child Care (Job Market Paper) [PDF]

Quality child care can improve low-income children's long-run outcomes. To increase access to high-quality care, the largest child care program in the United States gives states $12.4 billion annually to fund child care subsidy programs. Given a fixed budget, states trade off serving more families against giving larger subsidies to fewer families. It is unclear which allocation is better for eligible families. I first show that market quality and prices change with the number of subsidized families and the maximum subsidy. To identify the effect of each, I use variation in application fixed costs across space and in the maximum subsidy across time, space, and provider type. I find that a higher maximum subsidy increases average out-of-pocket payments and subsidizing more local families increases the probability that a neighborhood has a higher-quality provider. Motivated by these findings, I estimate a model of the child care market with endogenous prices, quality, and number of subsidized families. I use the model to evaluate a reform Wisconsin adopted in 2022 after a federal budget increase. I compare the reform, which increased the maximum subsidy by 130% on average, against using the same budget to offer smaller subsidies to more families. Under the larger subsidy, 17% more low-income children enroll in either public care or high-quality private care relative to subsidizing more families. However, 19% more eligible children forgo formal care overall. Higher prices explain 61% of this decline. As a result, consumer surplus for eligible families is 17% lower and producer surplus is 2% lower.

The Effect of Childcare on Female Labor Supply and Child Development in Low and Middle Income Countries [PDF]

with Costas Meghir

Child care services can offer an opportunity for women to develop their careers and increase household income. At the same time, it can affect the development of children, depending on the quality of care. We first develop a collective model of the household as a conceptual framework to show how improving the availability of child care can affect mothers' labor market opportunities and earnings and how it can affect child development. We then review the literature on the effects of child care on the employment and earnings of women and on child development. Child care can increase mothers' employment. However, limited work opportunities or strict norms against maternal work can keep mothers out of the labor force. When other family members provide care, child care can free these caregivers to work or attend school. When studies measure the impact on child development, the results are mixed and depend on both context and quality of provision.
